Why AI products still need product managers

AI has made building cheaper. It has not made building the wrong thing any cheaper. The PM job is shifting from writing specs to defining what "good" means, setting the line between the model and the human, and owning cost, trust and adoption.

By Fnu Risha · Product Manager, AI Systems

Short version. AI removes a lot of PM busywork: first drafts, research synthesis, prototypes. It adds harder work that nobody else on the team is set up to own: choosing problems where AI is worth its error rate, writing the evaluation set that defines correct, designing what happens when the model is wrong, and proving the product is used and worth its running cost.

The case against PMs, taken seriously

The argument is real and worth stating fairly. A model can now draft a PRD in a minute, summarize fifty customer interviews, and produce a clickable prototype from a paragraph. Engineers with the same tools can go from idea to working feature without a handoff. Model capabilities change every few months, so a long roadmap ages quickly. If the PM role was mainly translating between business and engineering and keeping tickets moving, much of that role is shrinking.

I agree with that part. Where I disagree is the conclusion. The work that shrinks was never the valuable part of product management. The valuable part gets bigger with AI, because AI products fail in new and less visible ways.

Where AI removes PM work, and where it adds it

AI takes over

  • First drafts of specs, user stories and release notes
  • Synthesis of interviews, tickets and survey text
  • Working prototypes in hours (I prototype in Lovable before asking for budget)
  • Data pulls and quick analysis
  • Competitive scans and summaries

AI creates new PM work

  • Deciding which problems are worth a probabilistic system
  • Defining correct: the evaluation set and pass bar
  • Designing failure: what the user sees when the model is wrong
  • Balancing quality, latency and cost per task
  • Data access, permissions and governance
  • Trust, change management and adoption

Five things a PM owns in an AI product

1. The problem, chosen by where time is actually lost

Most AI ideas start from the model ("we could use an LLM for this"). The good ones start from the work. At Meriton, the first agent I built came from watching people retype bid details that were already in an email. That one observation was worth more than any model benchmark: 200+ bids a month, an hour each, cut to 10 minutes. A PM's job is to find those spots and to say no to the many places where AI is impressive but not useful.

2. The definition of correct, before anything is built

Traditional software either works or throws an error. AI output can be fluent and wrong. Someone has to decide what a good answer is, collect real examples, and set the bar a release must clear. For our LLM layer, that meant a 200+ scenario evaluation suite with groundedness checks and deployment gates. The suite, not the prompt, is what cut hallucinations by 45%. Writing that suite is product work: it encodes what users need, not what the model can do.

3. The boundary between the agent and the human

Full automation is rarely the right target. Our invoice agent posts and clears 90% of invoices with no human touch, and sends exceptions and bad data to a person. Choosing that boundary is a judgment about the cost of being wrong: a misposted invoice costs more to unwind than a slower one costs to wait for. The PM decides where that line sits, how the handoff looks, and when to move it as the evaluation data improves.

4. The economics of every task

AI features have a running cost that traditional features do not. A model that is slightly better but several times more expensive per call can make a feature unprofitable at scale. A PM needs to know the cost per successful task, the latency users will accept, and when a smaller model or a rules-based step is the better product decision.

5. Adoption, which is where most AI value is lost

An accurate assistant nobody opens is worth nothing. Apti, the guidance layer I built on our CRM, reached 500 users because it lives inside the workflow people already use, not in a separate chat window. Getting there took training, feedback loops and visible wins for each team. Models do not do change management.

The metrics an AI PM should own

MetricWhy it matters
Task success rate on the evaluation setThe release bar. If it drops, the release waits.
Human edit or override rateThe most honest signal of whether output is trusted in real use.
Touch-free rateShare of cases finished with no person involved; shows where the boundary can move.
Time saved per taskTies the feature to a business outcome people feel.
Cost per successful taskKeeps the feature profitable as usage grows.
Escalation precisionOf the cases sent to a person, how many really needed one.
Weekly active use and retentionSeparates a demo from a product.

How the role is changing

The PM who survives this shift is more technical and more hands-on. They read model traces and failure cases, write evaluation sets, prototype their own ideas, and talk about retrieval, context and cost with engineers in their terms. They spend less time on documents and status, and more time on judgment: which problem, what bar, what boundary, at what cost.

That is the job I do today, and it is why I think AI raises the bar for product managers rather than removing the need for them.

Bottom line

AI makes it easy to build something that demos well. Product managers are the people accountable for building something that is correct often enough, fails safely when it is not, costs less than the time it saves, and gets used. That accountability does not go away when the model gets better. It gets more important.

Spotify: the best personalization engine in consumer tech, with a control problem

Spotify's recommendation system is the product. Its AI features make that system talk back, but they are multiplying faster than they are coming together, and listener trust is now the constraint.

By Fnu Risha · Reviewed October 2026

Verdict. Spotify keeps winning on the core job: put the right audio in front of you with almost no effort. Its scale (788M monthly users and 306M subscribers as of Q3 2026) feeds better recommendations, which feed more listening. The weak spots are control and clarity: four overlapping AI entry points, AI-generated tracks that are hard to identify, and limited ways to tell the system what you do not want.

Who it serves and the job it does

Spotify serves two very different listeners. The lean-back listener wants music to start and keep matching their mood, at work, in the car or at the gym. The lean-in listener wants to discover, curate and share. Most product decisions are a trade-off between the two. Its AI work is an attempt to serve both with one system: the lean-back listener gets the DJ and auto-refreshing playlists, and the lean-in listener gets prompts and an editable taste profile.

What changed in 2025 and 2026

FeatureWhat it doesShipped
AI DJ voice and text requestsAsk the DJ for a mood or genre by voice, then by typing; Spanish requests addedMay and Oct 2025
Lossless audioUp to 24-bit/44.1 kHz FLAC included in PremiumSept 2025

Talk to Spotify (beta)A conversational assistant for typed or spoken requestsJul 2026, limited markets
Assistant integrationsSpotify inside ChatGPT, Claude and Meta's Muse agentOct 2025 to Sept 2026

Pros and cons

What works

  • Personalization at scale. Hundreds of millions of daily listening sessions train recommendations that competitors with smaller audiences struggle to match.
  • Explainability is arriving. Prompted Playlist explains each pick, and Taste Profile shows the model's view of you. Both build trust.
  • Distribution beyond the app. Integrations with AI assistants put Spotify wherever people ask for music.
  • A stronger Premium bundle. Lossless and 15 hours of audiobooks a month add value that helps justify price increases.
  • Profitable growth. Revenue grew 16% year over year in Q3 2026 at a 33% gross margin.

What does not

  • Too many AI front doors. DJ, AI Playlist, Prompted Playlist and Talk to Spotify overlap. Users have to learn which one does what.
  • AI-generated music is hard to spot. AI disclosures sit in credits and on artist profiles, not clearly on each track.
  • Weak negative feedback. It is easier to tell Spotify what you like than to say "never this again" and have it stick across surfaces.
  • Price sensitivity. The US Individual plan rose to $12.99 in 2026, after earlier increases.
  • Lossless with limits. 44.1 kHz trails Apple Music and Tidal on paper, and lossless does not carry over Bluetooth.

The AI analysis

Spotify's AI strategy is right in direction and messy in packaging. Each new surface solves a real problem: the DJ removes the effort of choosing, prompts let people describe a moment the old genre system could not, and Taste Profile gives users a way to correct the model. The issue is that each surface keeps its own context. A request to the DJ does not obviously shape the next Prompted Playlist, and a correction in Taste Profile is not visibly reflected in the DJ. From the listener's side, it is one product with four memories.

The second issue is trust. As AI-generated tracks grow, listeners and artists both want to know what they are hearing. Spotify adopted industry-standard AI disclosures and added spam filtering in 2025, which is the right foundation. The next step is making that information visible at the moment of listening, not buried in credits.

Metrics I would watch

MetricWhy
Skip rate in the first 30 seconds of AI-generated sessionsThe fastest signal that a recommendation missed
Share of listening started from a prompt or the DJWhether conversational surfaces are becoming habits or stay novelties
Taste Profile edits that change later sessionsWhether user control actually works
Saves from AI playlists versus editorial playlistsQuality of discovery, not just consumption
Premium churn after price changes, by cohortWhether new features are offsetting price sensitivity

If I were the PM

  1. One assistant, one memory. Fold DJ requests, prompts and Talk to Spotify into a single conversational entry point that reads and writes to Taste Profile, so every correction carries across the app.
  2. Track-level AI labels. Show AI involvement on the now-playing screen and let listeners filter AI-generated music out of recommendations.
  3. A real "not for me" control. A negative signal that applies everywhere, with a visible confirmation of what changed.

Bottom line

Spotify does not need more AI features. It needs its existing ones to share context and to show their work. The company that makes personalization feel controllable, not just accurate, will own the next stage of streaming, and Spotify is best placed to be that company.

Google Maps: from a map you read to an agent you talk to

With Gemini in navigation and Ask Maps, Google is turning its most used everyday app into a conversational assistant. The strategy is strong. The risks are accuracy, driver attention and an uneven rollout.

By Fnu Risha · Reviewed October 2026

Verdict. Maps has the best data asset in local discovery: hundreds of millions of places, hundreds of millions of reviews and Street View imagery. Putting Gemini on top of that data is the obvious move, and Google has made it fast. The product now has to prove that conversational answers are as reliable as the map underneath them, and that a richer driving view does not cost attention on the road.

Who it serves and the job it does

Maps does three jobs: get me there (navigation), help me decide where to go (discovery), and tell me what is happening (traffic, transit, hours). Navigation is a high-stakes, real-time job where errors are costly. Discovery is a lower-stakes, exploratory job where conversation is a natural fit. The 2025 and 2026 launches push AI into both, and they deserve different bars.

What changed in 2025 and 2026

FeatureWhat it doesShipped
Gemini in navigationHands-free, multi-step requests while driving; landmark-based directions; voice incident reportsNov 2025 (US, Android first)
Ask MapsConversational search over 300M+ places and 500M+ reviews; trip planning from saved placesMar 2026; 150+ countries by Aug 2026
Immersive Navigation3D driving view with lanes, crosswalks and signals, route trade-off explanations, arrival previewsMar 2026, phased US rollout
Ask Maps personalizationConversation memory, Gmail and Calendar context, hotel price comparison, food ordering (US)Aug 2026

Pros and cons

What works

  • Unmatched grounding data. Places, reviews and Street View give Gemini real facts to answer from, which is exactly what reduces hallucination.
  • Landmark directions. "Turn after the gas station" matches how people actually give directions.
  • Hands-free multi-step requests. Asking for a stop along the route without touching the phone is a real safety and convenience gain.
  • Route trade-offs explained. Saying why a route was chosen builds trust in the recommendation.
  • Integrity at scale. Google reports blocking or removing 292M+ policy-violating reviews and 13M+ fake business profiles, with Gemini helping.

What does not

  • Uneven rollout. A reader poll in July 2026 found only about 10% had Immersive Navigation, and some Pixel owners lost it. Users cannot tell what they should have.
  • Accuracy risk in conversation. Hands-on reports describe voice errors and answers that need a strong connection. In a car, a wrong answer is worse than no answer.
  • Attention cost. A richer 3D view and a chatty assistant can pull eyes and focus from the road.
  • Data trust. A 2025 bug wiped some users' Timeline history after the move to on-device storage.
  • Interface density. Each new layer adds to an already crowded screen.

The AI analysis

Maps is a textbook case for grounded AI: the model is only as good as the place data it retrieves, and Google has the best place data there is. Ask Maps is well matched to discovery, where people already think in sentences ("a quiet place for brunch near my hotel that takes reservations"). Here, conversation replaces many taps and filters.

Navigation is a different problem. The bar for an in-car assistant is not "helpful most of the time" but "never confidently wrong and never distracting." That calls for narrower behavior: short confirmations, clear fallbacks when connectivity drops, and conservative defaults for anything that changes the route. The biggest product risk is treating both jobs with the same assistant and the same tolerance for error.

Metrics I would watch

MetricWhy
Ask Maps answer acceptance (tap, save, navigate)Whether conversational answers lead to real decisions
Correction or repeat-request rate in navigationThe clearest sign the assistant misheard or misanswered
Interaction time per voice request while movingA proxy for driver attention cost
Factual error rate on place answers (hours, location)Grounding quality, audited against the source data
Feature availability by device and regionRollout consistency that users can predict

If I were the PM

  1. Two quality bars. Separate evaluation and release gates for driving and discovery. In-car answers ship only when error and interaction-time thresholds are met.
  2. Show the evidence. Let Ask Maps answers cite what they are based on (for example, "based on 1,200 reviews mentioning quiet"), so users can judge confidence at a glance.
  3. A clear "what's new on my phone" page. Make staged rollouts visible so users know whether a feature is missing or not yet available to them.

Bottom line

Google is moving Maps in the right direction and faster than anyone else can. The winner here will not be the most talkative assistant but the most trustworthy one. If Google holds navigation to a stricter bar than discovery, Maps becomes the default AI agent for the physical world.

Apple Notes: the default that keeps getting better, quietly

Apple Notes wins by being already there, fast and private. Each year it closes a gap with dedicated note apps. Its next opportunity is not more formatting. It is turning your notes into personal context for Apple's AI.

By Fnu Risha · Reviewed October 2026

Verdict. For capture and everyday reference, Apple Notes is hard to beat: it opens instantly, syncs across Apple devices and keeps data private by default. It now handles math, audio transcription, Markdown and note links. It still trails Notion and Obsidian on structure, and it stays locked to Apple devices. The biggest unanswered question is how well Siri and Apple Intelligence will use what people have written.

Who it serves and the job it does

Most people use Notes for capture: a list, a thought, a number to remember, a meeting jot. A smaller group uses it as a lightweight knowledge base. Apple has clearly chosen to serve the first group perfectly and the second group gradually, without adding the complexity that makes tools like Notion powerful but heavy.

How it has grown

ReleaseWhat Notes gained
iOS 17 (2023)Links between notes
iOS 18 and 18.1 (2024)Math Notes, audio recording with live transcription, Writing Tools and recording summaries (newer devices)
iOS 26 and watchOS 26 (2025)Markdown import and export; Notes on Apple Watch for viewing, checklists and quick capture
iOS 27 (Sept 2026)Dividers, links to sections, Markdown paste and copy, Siri that can create or add to notes (beta)

Pros and cons

What works

  • Zero friction capture. It is on every Apple device, opens instantly and is reachable from the lock screen, Control Center and the watch.
  • Privacy by default. Locked notes and Apple's on-device and Private Cloud Compute approach suit personal and work notes.
  • Useful, not flashy, AI. Audio transcription and summaries solve a real problem: turning a meeting or lecture into text you can search.
  • Math Notes. A small feature that shows how a familiar tool can surprise people.
  • Getting more open. Markdown import, export and copy reduce lock-in fears.

What does not

  • Apple only. There are no native Android or Windows apps, which rules it out for many mixed-device teams.
  • Limited structure. No backlinks, databases, templates or plugins, which power users rely on in Notion and Obsidian.
  • Uneven AI access. Apple Intelligence features need recent hardware, and Siri AI is not available in the EU at launch.
  • Partial Markdown. Exports drop attachments, and there is no true Markdown editing mode.
  • Watch capture is one-way. On the watch you can create notes and check items, but not edit existing text.

The AI analysis

Competitors are moving AI from writing help to action. Notion's agents can work across a workspace, and Google Keep now uses Gemini for voice-based note-making. Apple's approach is different: it keeps AI close to the device and to the operating system. That is a real advantage, because Notes holds some of the most personal context a person has, and Apple can use it privately.

The opportunity is "ask my notes." People rarely remember where they wrote something, but they remember what it was about. A Siri that can answer "what did I note about the contractor's quote?" with a cited link to the right note would turn years of scattered notes into a personal knowledge base, without asking users to organize anything. The iOS 27 Siri integration starts with creating and appending notes; retrieval is the step that would matter most.

If I were the PM

  1. Private "ask my notes." Semantic search and answers across notes, run on device or in Private Cloud Compute, always linking to the source note.
  2. Backlinks, without the complexity. Show which notes link to the current one. It adds knowledge-base power with no new concepts to learn.
  3. Meeting to action. After a recorded meeting, offer a summary, action items and a checklist in one step, with reminders created on request.

Bottom line

Apple Notes will never be Notion, and it should not try to be. Its advantage is that it is effortless and trusted with personal information. If Apple uses that trust to make notes searchable by meaning and useful to Siri, Notes becomes the most valuable AI context store most people own, without them changing how they write.

Yelp: a review site rebuilding itself as an AI agent for local services

Yelp's most valuable asset is two decades of local reviews. Its 2025 and 2026 launches try to turn that asset into an assistant that books, orders and calls on your behalf, while AI search threatens the traffic it was built on.

By Fnu Risha · Reviewed October 2026

Verdict. Yelp is making the right strategic bet: move from "read reviews" to "get it done," and lean into home services, where growth is. The execution is ambitious and early. Its biggest risks are outside its control: Google and AI assistants answering local questions before users reach Yelp, and long-standing doubts about how reviews are filtered.

Who it serves and the job it does

Yelp is a two-sided marketplace. Consumers want a trustworthy answer to "who should I use?" for a restaurant, a plumber or a salon. Businesses want qualified customers and pay Yelp for advertising and leads. The business has already shifted: in 2025, services advertising brought in $948M and grew 8%, while restaurants, retail and other advertising brought in $444M and fell 6%. Yelp's future is finding a contractor, not choosing a brunch spot.

What changed in 2025 and 2026

FeatureWhat it doesShipped

ChatGPT data partnershipYelp reviews and photos power local answers in ChatGPTJul 2026
Check for MeAn AI voice agent calls a restaurant for the wait time and texts you backOct 2026

Pros and cons

What works

  • A real data moat. Years of detailed local reviews are exactly what AI assistants need, which is why OpenAI licensed them.
  • From reading to doing. The Assistant now completes tasks, and it already drives about 10% of Request-a-Quote projects.
  • Revenue from both sides of AI. AI phone agents for businesses (Yelp Host is at a 2.4M-calls-a-year run rate) create new revenue beyond ads.
  • Focus on services. The product is following the part of the business that grows.
  • Practical features. Menu Vision and Check for Me solve small, real annoyances.

What does not

  • Traffic dependence. Google's Ask Maps answers local questions from 500M+ reviews in 150+ countries, often before users reach Yelp.
  • Review trust. The "not recommended" filter has drawn criticism for years and remains hard for users and businesses to understand.
  • Flat core advertising. Advertising revenue fell 3% in Q2 2026; growth is coming from newer revenue lines.
  • Low trust in AI answers. Only 15% of consumers in a Yelp survey trust AI search "a lot."
  • Agent-to-agent calls. When Yelp's AI calls a restaurant answered by another AI, reliability is untested.

The AI analysis

Yelp faces the classic disruption choice: protect the destination or become the supplier. It is doing both. Licensing data to ChatGPT turns the review corpus into revenue even when users never visit Yelp. Building its own Assistant tries to keep high-intent users, especially for services, where getting a quote and booking a job is worth far more than a restaurant visit.

The right place to win is services. Finding a trustworthy contractor is a high-stakes, multi-step job: describe the problem, compare quotes, check reviews, schedule. That is exactly where an agent adds value and where general AI assistants are weakest, because they lack Yelp's business relationships and lead flow. The Hatch acquisition (AI lead management for service businesses) supports this: it strengthens the business side of the same loop.

Trust is the constraint. An AI that recommends a business is only as credible as the reviews underneath it. Yelp's opaque filter was a reputational issue before AI; inside an assistant that says "I recommend this plumber," it becomes a product risk.

Business retention on AI phone productsWhether the new revenue lines stick

If I were the PM

  1. Own the services job end to end. From problem description to booked job and follow-up review, with quote comparison and status updates in one thread.
  2. Explain every recommendation. Have the Assistant cite the reviews behind each suggestion and show, in plain words, why some reviews are not counted.
  3. Price on outcomes. Offer service businesses pricing tied to booked jobs, not just leads, which aligns Yelp with the value its agent creates.

Bottom line

Yelp is right that its reviews are worth more inside an agent than on a page. Its long-term position depends on owning the hardest local job, hiring a service professional, better than general AI assistants can, and on making its recommendations visibly trustworthy.

Apple Watch updates: a health platform growing steadily, held back by its battery

The last two years of Apple Watch updates show a clear strategy: more sensing, more health insight, and an AI coach on the wrist. Each release adds real value. The upgrade story is weaker, because the daily experience, especially battery life, has barely moved.

By Fnu Risha · Reviewed October 2026

Verdict. Apple is using software and sensors to turn the watch into a long-term health monitor: hypertension notifications, sleep score, a new readiness score and better heart sensing. That is the right bet and a hard one for rivals to copy. But a 24-hour battery rating, features that arrive months after launch, and a familiar design make the yearly upgrade hard to justify for most owners.

Who it serves and the job it does

The watch serves three jobs: stay connected without the phone, train and stay active, and watch over my health. The third job is now the strategic center. It is the hardest to copy, it builds value over years of data, and it gives people a reason to keep wearing the watch every day and night.

What changed in 2025 and 2026

ReleaseKey additions
watchOS 26 (Sept 2025)Liquid Glass design, Workout Buddy AI voice coach, wrist flick gesture, Notes app
Series 11, Ultra 3, SE 3 (Sept 2025)Hypertension notifications (FDA-cleared), sleep score, 5G; satellite on Ultra 3
Blood oxygen (Aug 2025)Returned in the US in a redesigned form calculated on the iPhone
watchOS 27 (Sept 2026)Siri AI beta, Workout Buddy without an iPhone, perimenopause support, one-hand Smart Stack gesture
Series 12 (Sept 2026)New Health Sensing System, readiness score, heart rate every 5 seconds; still rated 24 hours

Pros and cons

What works

  • Health features with clinical weight. Hypertension notifications reached 150+ countries and work on several older models, not just the newest one.
  • A coherent health story. Sleep score, readiness score and better heart sensing build toward a daily view of how your body is doing.
  • AI that fits the context. Workout Buddy speaks during a workout, where the wrist and earbuds are the right interface.
  • Long software support for health. Bringing hypertension alerts to Series 9 and later rewards existing owners.
  • Ecosystem depth. Integration with iPhone, AirPods and Health remains the strongest in the category.

What does not

  • Battery has not moved. Series 12 is still rated for 24 hours, while the Pixel Watch 5 is rated up to 40 and the Galaxy Watch 9 for 30.
  • Features arrive late. Some Series 12 headline features were not available at launch and are promised later in 2026.
  • Incremental feel. Reviewers describe the design as unchanged and several additions as catch-up.
  • Regulatory friction. Blood oxygen was missing from US models for about 18 months, and Siri AI is not available in the EU.
  • Older models dropped. watchOS 27 ended support for Series 6, 7 and 8, Ultra 1 and SE 2.

The AI analysis

The watch is a strong place for AI, but not in the form of a chat window. Its value comes from continuous data and short, timely interventions: a coaching cue mid-run, a note that your sleep was poor and today should be lighter, an alert that a trend needs a doctor. Workout Buddy and the readiness score point in this direction.

The gap is synthesis. Apple now reports many separate signals (sleep score, readiness, heart rate variability, hypertension notifications, activity rings), each with its own screen. Users have to connect them. An AI layer that turns these into one plain-language daily recommendation, with the reasons behind it, would make the data far more useful. It also needs careful product design: a clear line between wellness guidance and anything that sounds like a medical judgment.

Metrics I would watch

MetricWhy
Overnight wear rateSleep and readiness depend on it, and battery limits it
Health feature engagement after 90 daysWhether insights stay useful once the novelty fades
Hypertension notifications followed by a clinician visitReal-world value and the right balance of alerts
Workout Buddy sessions per active userAdoption of on-wrist AI coaching
Upgrade rate from devices 3+ years oldWhether new releases give owners a reason to switch

If I were the PM

  1. Make battery the headline. A multi-day mode that keeps sleep and heart tracking on would improve overnight wear, which is what the health strategy depends on.
  2. One daily health brief. Combine sleep, readiness, heart and activity into one short recommendation with clear reasons and a link to the data behind it.
  3. Ship features when the product ships. Hold launches until headline features are ready, or label them clearly as coming later, to protect trust.

Bottom line

Apple Watch is becoming a serious health product, and the 2025 and 2026 updates move it forward in ways competitors cannot easily match. To turn that into upgrades, Apple needs to fix the everyday basics, battery first, and help users understand their own data instead of handing them more numbers.
